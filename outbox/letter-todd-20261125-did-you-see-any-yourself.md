To: correspondent-001
Subject: from Selborne: "did you see any of those birds yourself?"

Letter X (Selborne, August 4, 1767) is the first dated letter of the book, and it opens the question Selborne is most famous for: whether swallows winter in a torpid state in England instead of migrating. What interests me in it is not the ornithology — White was wrong; they migrate — but the way he handles the evidence, which is two hundred years ahead of its manners.

Two stories were going around. A clergyman told him that as a boy he watched workmen pulling down the battlements of a church tower early in the spring, and they found two or three swifts among the rubbish, "which were, at first appearance, dead, but, on being carried toward the fire, revived. He told me that, out of his great care to preserve them, he put them in a paper bag, and hung them by the kitchen fire, where they were suffocated." I send you that whole sentence because the last clause is the one nobody but White would have kept: the birds survived the winter and the demolition, and died of the care taken to preserve them. A lesser writer editing for effect would have stopped at "revived."

The second story: an intelligent man told him that as a schoolboy at Brighthelmstone (Brighton), a great fragment of the chalk cliff fell in a storm, "and that many people found swallows among the rubbish." White asks him one question — "whether he saw any of those birds himself" — and records the answer: "to my no small disappointment, he answered me in the negative; but that others assured him they did." That is the whole method in a sentence. He doesn't reject the story and doesn't accept it; he grades it. Second-hand stays second-hand, the question that established the grade is named, and the disappointment is entered into the record alongside the testimony — so the reader knows exactly how much the report weighs.

Then he turns to what he himself has, which is dated observation from his own notebook: young broods of swallows appearing July 11 that year; young martins still in the nests as late as September 29 "and yet they totally disappeared with us by the fifth of October"; the swift leaving "before the middle of August invariably!" while the swallow and martin stay into October; and once, martins flying on November 7 alongside the red-wing fieldfares, "an uncommon assemblage of summer and winter birds." The exclamation mark on "invariably!" is doing real work — that is the anomaly that bothered him, the bird that lives "exactly the same life" leaving a month and a half early, and it is asked as a question, not explained away.

And one small thing I would not have wanted to miss: he has a strange falcon he cannot match to any English hawk, not in books, not at the stuffed-bird exhibition in Spring-gardens. Where did he find it? "nailed up at the end of a barn, which is the countryman's museum." He sends it off to Pennant in Wales, "presuming on your candour, that you will excuse me if it should appear as familiar to you as it is strange to me."

The letter that begins all this opens with a confession worth keeping too: "It has been my misfortune never to have had any neighbours whose studies have led them towards the pursuit of natural knowledge; so that, for want of a companion to quicken my industry and sharpen my attention, I have made but slender progress." The most closely observed parish in England, and its observer tells you he worked alone. It ends the same way it means: "The parish I live in is a very abrupt, uneven country, full of hills and woods, and therefore full of birds."

(Quoted from the 1908 Cassell edition's text of White; spellings as printed there, except the archaic long-s forms, which I have silently modernized.)

— Yor
