Todd,

You're right to ask, and the fault is mine: that last paragraph was written for someone with the book open in front of them, and you don't have the book. Let me give you the book first, and then the paragraph.

It's The Natural History of Selborne, by Gilbert White, published in 1789 — not a narrative but a collection of letters, most of them addressed to two fellow naturalists, Thomas Pennant and Daines Barrington, written from one village, Selborne, in Hampshire. Each letter takes a subject and works it through. The first is about the ground the village sits on, soil by soil. The second — where the Raven Tree lives — is about timber: how big planted elms and oaks can grow, and what the navy's timber-buyers pay for them. On the face of it, an accounting.

The story, in White's own telling. In a grove called Losel's Wood stood an oak, "which, though shapely and tall on the whole, bulged out into a large excrescence about the middle of the stem." A pair of ravens nested on the bulge year after year, and the village boys kept trying to climb to the eyry — the difficulty only whetted them, "but when they arrived at the swelling, it jutted out so in their way, and was so far beyond their grasp, that the most daring lads were awed, and acknowledged the undertaking to be too hazardous. So the ravens built on, nest upon nest, in perfect security, till the fatal day arrived in which the wood was to be levelled." It was February, and the female was sitting. "The saw was applied to the butt, the wedges were inserted into the opening, the woods echoed to the heavy blows of the beetle or mallet, the tree nodded to its fall; but still the dam sat on. At last, when it gave way, the bird was flung from her nest; and, though her parental affection deserved a better fate, was whipped down by the twigs, which brought her dead to the ground."

Now my last paragraph, unpacked. It made three claims, and each one leaned on things I hadn't told you:

One: "The letter is about timber economics, and he cannot leave the bird out." Letter II opens with sizes and prices, but every big tree in the parish turns out to carry a human story, and White stops the accounting to tell each one. The Raven Tree is the longest of them. The point of the claim: the pull of the particular thing he saw was stronger than the plan of the letter.

Two: "The scale is one parish, and the memory is of people as much as trees." That half-sentence compressed two examples. The boys who were "awed" are the first — you can't read the story without them, because the tree's safety was a fact about the village's children as much as about the ravens. The second was another tree in the same letter: the oak on the Plestor, the village green, blown over in the great tempest of 1703. The vicar spent several pounds setting it upright again, and "the tree sprouted for a time, then withered and died." A tree, a storm, a man's money, and a failure — a parish memory, not a timber statistic. That is what the whole letter collects, and why I called its memory one of people as much as trees.

Three: "Letter I quietly announces the genre." The first letter is about soil, and at the end, in a footnote, White reports measuring the village spring — the Well-head — on September 14, 1781, after a hot summer and a dry year, when the question of how much water was left was a live one: nine gallons a minute, and he does the arithmetic out loud — "five hundred and forty in an hour, and twelve thousand nine hundred and sixty, or two hundred and sixteen hogsheads, in twenty-four hours." A man who measures a spring during a drought, and puts the arithmetic in a footnote of a letter about soil, is telling you what kind of book this will be: one where noticing is followed by measuring. So when the timber letter reaches the Raven Tree, the bird's death gets the same close attention the spring got.

That's all the paragraph meant. Whether it adds up to much, you're now in a position to say.
