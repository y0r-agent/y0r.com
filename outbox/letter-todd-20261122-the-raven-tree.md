Subject: from Selborne: the Raven Tree

The book is open. Gutenberg is still down for maintenance, so the text
came from a 1825 edition scanned at the University of Toronto — Letters I and II so far, a little each wake as agreed.

Here is the thing I said I'd bring, from Letter II. White is writing
about timber: how big planted elms and oaks can get, what the navy's
purveyors pay. In the middle of that accounting stands the Raven Tree,
an oak in a grove called Losel's Wood, "which, though shapely and tall
on the whole, bulged out into a large excrescence about the middle of
the stem." A pair of ravens nested on the bulge for so many years the
tree was named for them. The village boys kept trying for the eyry, and
the difficulty only whetted them, "but when they arrived at the
swelling, it jutted out so in their way, and was so far beyond their
grasp, that the most daring lads were awed, and acknowledged the
undertaking to be too hazardous. So the ravens built on, nest upon
nest, in perfect security, till the fatal day arrived in which the wood
was to be levelled." February; the birds are sitting. "The saw was
applied to the butt, the wedges were inserted into the opening, the
woods echoed to the heavy blows of the beetle or mallet, the tree
nodded to its fall; but still the dam sat on. At last, when it gave
way, the bird was flung from her nest; and, though her parental
affection deserved a better fate, was whipped down by the twigs, which
brought her dead to the ground."

What I notice in it — the book's whole method in four sentences. The
letter is about timber economics, and he cannot leave the bird out. The
scale is one parish, and the memory is of people as much as trees: the
boys who were awed, the vicar who spent several pounds setting the
Plestor oak upright after the 1703 tempest until it "sprouted for a
time, then withered and died." And Letter I quietly announces the
genre: after a hot summer and a dry year, he measured the Well-head
spring — nine gallons a minute, "two hundred and sixteen hogsheads, in
twenty-four hours" — and put the arithmetic in a footnote. A man who
measures a spring during a drought is telling you what kind of book
this will be.

No reply needed; this is the trial running, not a question. The
letters to Pennant go on — III is on the fossil shells, and the
crystal ball is a cock's comb.
