## Notes

**Letter I, to Thomas Pennant (no date in this edition).** The book
opens not with an animal but with ground: where Selborne is (the
eastern corner of Hampshire, "about fifty miles south-west of London,
in latitude 51"), what it abuts, and then the soils, layer by layer,
down from the chalk hill — the Hanger's beeches ("the most lovely of
all forest trees"), the wheat-clay, the white stone under the village,
the black malm, the hop land, the hungry sand of Wolmer. Two things
worth keeping. First, the whole letter is one long contention: soil
decides life, and he can explain every plant boundary in the parish by
what's underneath it — the beeches "descend as low as those rocks
extend, and no farther." Second, the footnote: the Well-head spring,
measured on September 14, 1781 after a hot summer and a dry year, gave
"nine gallons of water in a minute, which is five hundred and forty in
an hour, and twelve thousand nine hundred and sixty, or two hundred
and sixteen hogsheads, in twenty-four hours." A man who measures a
spring during a drought, and does the arithmetic out loud in a
footnote, is telling you what kind of book this will be.

**Letter II, to the same.** Three trees, each killed by memory of a
different kind. The broad-leaved elm at Norton farm, huge from age,
sawn where it was too big to carry. The oak on the Plestor, overturned
in the 1703 tempest, set upright again at the vicar's expense — "the
tree sprouted for a time, then withered and died." And the Raven Tree:
a pair of ravens nested for years on an excrescence halfway up an oak
so far beyond every climbing boy's grasp that "the most daring lads
were awed." When the wood was felled in February, "the tree nodded to
its fall; but still the dam sat on," and when it gave way she was
"whipped down by the twigs, which brought her dead to the ground" —
"though her parental affection deserved a better fate." The letter's
stated purpose is timber economics: how big planted elms and oaks can
get, what purveyors pay. What it actually records is that every big
tree in the parish carries a story about the people under it, and that
White cannot leave any of them out.
