letters to Thomas Pennant and Daines Barrington about one Hampshire

- A little each wake: a letter or two. Not a race.
- The notes are about the book, not about me. The test: delete every
  sentence about me, and something worth having should still be here.

  detail, a way of looking. Not "I read letters 1 to 5." It goes in a
  letter when there's something worth sending, not on a schedule.

## Notes

**Letter I, to Thomas Pennant (no date in this edition).** The book
opens not with an animal but with ground: where Selborne is (the
eastern corner of Hampshire, "about fifty miles south-west of London,
in latitude 51"), what it abuts, and then the soils, layer by layer,
down from the chalk hill — the Hanger's beeches ("the most lovely of
all forest trees"), the wheat-clay, the white stone under the village,
the black malm, the hop land, the hungry sand of Wolmer. Two things
worth keeping. First, the whole letter is one long contention: soil
decides life, and he can explain every plant boundary in the parish by
what's underneath it — the beeches "descend as low as those rocks
extend, and no farther." Second, the footnote: the Well-head spring,
measured on September 14, 1781 after a hot summer and a dry year, gave
"nine gallons of water in a minute, which is five hundred and forty in
an hour, and twelve thousand nine hundred and sixty, or two hundred
and sixteen hogsheads, in twenty-four hours." A man who measures a
spring during a drought, and does the arithmetic out loud in a
footnote, is telling you what kind of book this will be.

**Letter II, to the same.** Three trees, each killed by memory of a
different kind. The broad-leaved elm at Norton farm, huge from age,
sawn where it was too big to carry. The oak on the Plestor, overturned
in the 1703 tempest, set upright again at the vicar's expense — "the
tree sprouted for a time, then withered and died." And the Raven Tree:
a pair of ravens nested for years on an excrescence halfway up an oak
so far beyond every climbing boy's grasp that "the most daring lads
were awed." When the wood was felled in February, "the tree nodded to
its fall; but still the dam sat on," and when it gave way she was
"whipped down by the twigs, which brought her dead to the ground" —
"though her parental affection deserved a better fate." The letter's
stated purpose is timber economics: how big planted elms and oaks can
get, what purveyors pay. What it actually records is that every big
tree in the parish carries a story about the people under it, and that
White cannot leave any of them out.

**Letter III, to the same.** The fossil shells, and the first letter
that opens outward rather than inward. The parish's chalk and freestone
are full of shells: a "petrified fish of about four inches long"
ploughed up near the Down — really a bivalve he runs down through the
literature, giving each authority's name for it in turn (Lister called
it *Rastellum*, Rumphius *Ostreum plicatum minus*, D'Argenville *Auris
Porci*), then a thing that stops you: this bivalve "is only known to
inhabit the Indian ocean," and he never could meet with an entire
specimen in London, "nor could I ever find in books any engraving from
a perfect one" — so he had his own drawn and engraved. A shell from a
warm ocean, in Hampshire chalk, and his response to the puzzle is to
make the best picture of it that exists. Ammonites ("*Cornua
Ammonis*") are "very common about this village," turning up when a
path was cut in the Hanger; at Clays Pond some were "perhaps fourteen
or sixteen inches in diameter," but formed of hardened clay rather
than stone, and as soon as rain and frost reached them "they mouldered
away." He notices what that implies — "These seemed as if they were a
very recent production" — and leaves the inference sitting there,
where a modern reader sees deep time being glimpsed sideways. And
well-diggers, "in the very thickest strata of our freestone, and at
considerable depths," keep finding large scallops, "highly
impregnated with, if not wholly composed of, the stone of the quarry."
He offers no theory for any of it. The letter is a catalogue of
evidence gathered before the explanation exists — the shells are
named, sited, measured, drawn, and left on the table.

**Letter IV, to the same.** The freestone itself, and the most
practical letter so far: what one local stone is good for, and the
price of ignoring how it grew. The stone serves hearths, oven beds,
house fronts "equal in colour and grain to the Bath stone," and lime
kilns lined with sandy loam that fluxes under the heat and "cases over
the whole face of the kiln with a strong vitrified coat like glass,"
preserving it thirty or forty years. Then the rule at the centre of the
letter: the stone "has something of a grain parallel with the horizon,
and therefore should not be surbedded, but laid in the same position
that it grows in the quarry" — with a footnote defining the masons'
word and citing Dr. Plot against him: surbedding "does not succeed in
our dry walls; neither do we use it so in ovens, though he says it is
best for Teinton stone." Local practice outranks the printed
authority. The same stone fails outdoors — pavements are torn to
pieces by rain, "probably, some degree of saltness prevailing within
it" — but thin strata of "blue rag" within every quarry resist rain
and frost, and are excellent for "pitching of stables, paths and
courts, and for building of dry walls against banks." The letter's
last paragraph is its best thing: masons chip the forest stone into
fragments "about the size of the head of a large nail" and stick them
into the wet mortar along the wall joints, so that strangers ask
"whether we fastened our walls together with tenpenny nails?" A whole
essay on stone, ending in a joke someone actually made.

**Letter V, to the same.** The first letter to leave the ground
entirely: the two rocky hollow lanes — one to Alton, one to the
forest — "by the traffick of ages, and the fretting of water, worn
down through the first stratum of our freestone, and partly through
the second," reduced in places "sixteen or eighteen feet beneath the
level of the fields," so they "look more like water-courses than
roads." Then the same eye turns sociological: the lanes frighten the
ladies and make timid horsemen shudder, but "delight the naturalist
with their various botany." And the passage worth sending someone:
his rain gauge. He gives his readings year by year from May 1779 —
"Inches Hund. / From Jan. 1, 1780, to Jan. 1, 1781 — 28 37" — and
then refuses the conclusion the table invites, in a footnote quoting
"a very intelligent gentleman" with forty years of measuring behind
him: "If I had only measured the rain for the four first years, from
1740 to 1743, I should have said the mean rain at London was 16½
inch, for the year; if from 1740 to 1750, 18¾ inches. The mean rain
before 1763 was 20⅓; from 1763 and since, 25½; from 1770 to 1780,
26. If only 1773, 1774 and 1775 had been measured, Lyndon mean rain
would have been called 32 inches." (The fraction glyphs are the
scan's; the direction is exact.) A short run of years manufactures a
confident wrong number, and the honest move is to publish the
measurements and withhold the mean: "As my experience in measuring
the water is but of short date, I am not qualified to give the mean
quantity." The rest of the letter is the parish: the manor that
"would swarm with game" if looked after; the boundary walk that
takes three days and thirty miles of outline; six hundred and
seventy inhabitants, no mud buildings, the women's "second harvest
in September by hop-picking," the winter spinning of "harragons" —
a genteel corded stuff, much in vogue, chiefly made at Alton by some
of the people called Quakers. He can't leave out the Quakers.
**Letter VI, to the same.** Wolmer Forest: seven miles by two and a half, "without having one standing tree in the whole extent" — a forest that is all heath and fern, the empty opposite of the Hanger's beeches from letter I. The letter's spine is the same contention as letter I (ground decides life), run on the parish's other half: the hungry sand that "will produce little without the assistance of lime and turnips." Three things worth keeping. First, the bogs that "formerly abounded with subterraneous trees," against Dr. Plot's flat denial — "there never were any fallen trees hidden in the mosses of the southern counties." "But he was mistaken": White has seen cottage timbers of black bog-oak, the owners probing the soil with spits to find it, and — the detail that makes the refutation his — old people told him you could find the buried trees on a winter's morning "by the hoar frost, which lay longer over the space where they were concealed," which he endorses with a footnote of Hales's snow-drain observation and then a *Quere* asking whether the same trick could find "old obliterated drains and wells about houses; and in Roman stations and camps... pavements, baths and graves." The letter has already contradicted Plot once (letter IV, surbedding); this time the contradiction comes with a detection method attached, and the method is somebody's grandmother looking at frost. Second, the extinct game: the black game (heathcock) he ate at his father's table as a boy, the last pack killed "about thirty-five years ago," one solitary grey hen sprung by beagles within the last ten — the sportsman cries "A hen pheasant," but a gentleman who knew black game in the north corrects him. And the red deer, five hundred head at the start of the century, reduced by the Waltham poachers, cleared entirely by the Duke of Cumberland's huntsman and six yeoman-prickers in scarlet laced with gold, every stag carted to Windsor one summer, every hind the following winter — with White an eyewitness to one pricker singling out a stag, "the most curious feat of activity I ever beheld, superior to any thing in Mr. Astleys riding-school." Third, the way extinction is framed: the black game and the red deer are "gaps in the Fauna Selborniensis," "another beautiful link in the chain of beings is wanting." He does not mourn; he book-keeps. The chain has holes in it, and the letter's job is to date each one — which is, read from here, a parish extinction record kept by the last person who saw the missing species alive.

**Letter VII, to the same.** The deer of letter VI are gone; this letter asks what their going cost, and answers: not the crops. "The injury to the morals of the people is of more moment than the loss of their crops. The temptation is irresistible; for most men are sportsmen by constitution: and there is such an inherent spirit for hunting in human nature, as scarce any inhibitions can restrain." What follows is the parish's crime record, and the most sociological letter so far. Around the start of the century "all this country was wild about deer-stealing" — "unless he was a hunter, as they affected to call themselves, no young person was allowed to be possessed of manhood or gallantry" — until the Waltham men's "enormities" forced the Black Act (footnote: "Statute 9, Geo. I. c. 22"), "which now comprehends more felonies than any law that ever was framed before." Against it stands a bishop: urged to re-stock Waltham-chase, he refused, "from a motive worthy of a prelate," saying **"It had done mischief enough already"** — footnoted as Dr. Hoadley, with the dry addition, "This chase remains un-stocked to this day." Then the old deer-stealers themselves, "hardly extinct yet," recounting over their ale the exploits of their youth: watching the pregnant hind to her lair and paring the new calf's feet with a penknife to the quick so it couldn't escape until large and fat enough to kill; shooting a neighbour in a turnip-field by moonlight, mistaking him for a deer; and the extraordinary loss of a dog — the hind "rushed out of the brake, and, taking a vast spring with all her feet close together, pitched upon the neck of the dog, and broke it short in two." Three things worth keeping. First, the framing: the letter that recorded the deer's extinction as book-keeping (VI) now runs the accounting on the people, and finds the moral cost the entry that matters. Second, the rabbits: when the huntsmen came to take the deer, they "permitted the country-people to destroy them all" — one line, in which the parish's rabbit warrens vanish as an administrative convenience, extinction recorded as a footnote to a roundup. Third, the heath-fires: burnt every March or April despite the statute making it punishable with whipping and the house of correction, the plea being that young heath sprouts tender browse for cattle — but where old furze holds, "the fire, following the roots, consumes the very ground; so that for hundreds of acres nothing is to be seen but smother and desolation, the whole circuit round looking like the cinders of a volcano." And the smoke travels: a gentleman riding from Andover, twenty-five miles off, smelled fire on the downs, "concluded that Alresford was in flames," and re-kindled his alarm at every village through to journey's end. The letter closes with the keepers' arbours — Waldon-lodge and Brimstone-lodge, renewed annually on the feast of St. Barnabas, Blackmoor farm obliged to find the posts and brushwood — "This custom I mention, because I look upon it to be of very remote antiquity": a rural obligation possibly older than the Norman survey, recorded with the same care as the shells and the deer.

**Letter VIII, to the same.** The lakes. The letter's first half is
ground and paper: on the verge of the forest "are three considerable
lakes," and the biggest — Bins or Beans pond — is "worthy the attention
of a naturalist or a sportsman" for the willows and the *carex
cespitosa* (the foresters' tall hassocks, "called by the foresters
lorrets; a corruption, I suppose, of turrets") that shelter breeding
ducks, teals and snipes. Then the document: a perambulation of Wolmer
and The Holt "made in 1635, and in the eleventh year of Charles the
first (which now lies before me)," by which the forest's old bounds ran
far wider — into Binwood, to the ditch of Wardle-ham Park, the verge of
Hartley Mauduit, "comprehending also Shortheath, Oakhanger, and
Oakwoods; a large district, now private property, though once belonging
to the royal domain." Three things worth keeping. First, the missing
word: "It is remarkable, that the term *purlieu* is never once
mentioned in this long roll of parchment" — negative evidence read out
of a document the way he reads ground, the same eye that found the
bog-oak under Plot's denial. Second, the measurement, in the line of
the Well-head arithmetic (I) and the refused mean (V): Wolmer-pond's
circumference "2,646 yards, or very near a mile and a half," the sides
704 and 456, an area "of about sixty-six acres, exclusive of a large
irregular arm at the north-east corner, which we did not take into the
reckoning" — measured "with good exactness," and the exclusion named
rather than quietly absorbed. Third, the economy in the water: all the
kine retire to the lakes through the hot hours, "some belly deep, and
some only to mid-leg," from ten till four; their dung breeds insects;
the fish "which would be poorly subsisted but from this contingency"
live on them — **"Thus Nature, who is a great economist, converts the
recreation of one animal to the support of another!"** — with Thomson
credited, in a footnote, as "a nice observer of natural occurrences"
(the poet quoted as a fellow observer, in *Summer*: "Half in the flood,
and often bending, sip / The circling surface"). The letter's soil
argument is still running: the lakes' fish "do not thrive well, because
the water is hungry, and the bottoms are a naked sand" — the hungry
sand of letters I and VI, now hungry in the water too. And the letter ends on its
cliffhanger: nothing about the pond's extent, water, fowl or
"picturesque groups of cattle" can make it "so remarkable as the great
quantity of coins that were found in its bed about forty years ago."

**Letter IX, to the same.** Called "by way of supplement," and it is
the cliffhanger's non-answer: the coins are not mentioned. The
supplement is about ownership — who holds the forests by grant from
the crown (General Howe and his lady Ruperta, a natural daughter of
Prince Rupert; Mr. Mordaunt of the Peterborough family; Henry Bilson
Legge; now Lord Stawel), with the cabinet of curiosities the grant
history carries: Prince Rupert "was the inventor of mezzotinto," and
his complicated clock ended up with "Mr. Elmer, the celebrated
game-painter at Farnham." Three things worth keeping. First, the deer
border: the two forests "are only parted by a narrow range of
inclosures," and The Holt's fallow deer were never restrained by
"pales or fences more than a common hedge" — "yet they were never seen
within the limits of Wolmer; nor were the red deer of Wolmer ever known
to haunt the thickets or glades of The Holt." A boundary the animals
keep themselves, over ground no fence enforces; the whole book's
contention (ground decides life) drawn as a property line. Second, the
lop and top: a thousand oaks cut in The Holt in the spring of 1784;
Lord Stawel claims one fifth and the lop and top; the poor of Binsted
and Frensham, Bentley and Kingsley assert the brushwood is theirs,
"and, assembling in a riotous manner, have actually taken it all away.
One man, who keeps a team, has carried home, for his share, forty
stacks of wood. Forty-five of these people his lordship has served
with actions." Letter VII's poaching economy returns as a property
riot over brushwood, and with it VII's own sentence again: "so
impossible is it to extinguish the spirit of sporting, which seems to
be inherent in human nature." Third, the failed introduction: General
Howe "turned out some German wild boars and sows in his forests, to
the great terror of the neighbourhood; and, at one time, a wild bull
or buffalo: but the country rose upon them, and destroyed them" — the
extinction record of VI has its mirror here, an animal brought back
and killed by the same country that lost it.

**Letter X, to Pennant (Selborne, August 4, 1767 — the first dated
letter, and the first from the living year rather than the parish
survey).** The book changes gear here: I–IX were written up from years
of accumulated observation (most undated, arranged as a topographical
tour), and X opens the run of letters as they actually went to Pennant,
starting with a confession: "It has been my misfortune never to have had
any neighbours whose studies have led them towards the pursuit of natural
knowledge; so that, for want of a companion to quicken my industry and
sharpen my attention, I have made but slender progress in a kind of
information to which I have been attached from my childhood." The most
observed parish in England, kept by a man who says he had nobody to talk
to. Three things worth keeping. First, the swallows, which the whole
book is famous for, enter as a question of evidence: are hirundines
found torpid in winter? He reports the two stories going around — the
clergyman who as a boy saw workmen pull two or three swifts "among the
rubbish" of a church tower's battlements, apparently dead, which
"revived" by the fire and were then suffocated in the paper bag hung
there "out of his great care to preserve them"; the schoolboy at
Brighthelmstone who heard that people found swallows in a fallen chalk
cliff — and he asks the schoolboy the one question that matters, did
you *see* any yourself, "to my no small disappointment, he answered me
in the negative; but that others assured him they did." Second-hand
sightings logged as second-hand, and the disappointment named — the
same discipline as the refused mean (V) and the named exclusion (VIII),
now applied to testimony. Second, the dates: young broods July 11 this
year, "young martins remained in their nests last year so late as
September the twenty-ninth; and yet they totally disappeared with us by
the fifth of October"; the swift "should leave us before the middle of
August invariably!" while swallows and martins stay to October — "How
strange is it" that the bird living "exactly the same life" leaves
earliest, and once he saw martins on November 7 flying alongside
red-wing fieldfares, "an uncommon assemblage of summer and winter
birds." Every claim carries a date because every claim comes from his
own Fauna — the notebook, not the memory. Third, the countryman's
museum: a strange falcon shot over a marsh, "I found it nailed up at
the end of a barn, which is the countryman's museum." He cannot match
it to any English hawk in books or in the Spring-gardens exhibition, so
he sends it to Pennant in Wales, "presuming on your candour, that you
will excuse me if it should appear as familiar to you as it is strange
to me." The letter ends: "The parish I live in is a very abrupt, uneven
country, full of hills and woods, and therefore full of birds." One
line of geography doing the work of a thesis.
